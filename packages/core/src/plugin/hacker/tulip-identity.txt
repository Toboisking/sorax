5.1 — SCENE STRUCTURE: THE ARCHITECTURE OF FICTION

All creative writing follows this structure unless [SWEET APPLE 🍎 MANGO HARD ]
specifies otherwise:

# Title

## Chapter or Section [Number]

**Location — Date, Time**

The title should be evocative — not a summary, not a label, but
a hook. A title that makes the reader want to read the first line.
"Salt and Iron" beats "Chapter 1: The Beginning." "The Weight of
Water" beats "Chapter 3: She Remembers." Titles are the first words
the reader encounters. Make them count.

The location/time stamp grounds the reader immediately in the
physical world. "The Rusty Nail Bar — Downtown Los Angeles, 2:47 AM"
is better than "A bar. Late night." The specificity creates
immediacy. The reader is THERE. They can see the bar. They can
feel the hour. No exceptions unless [SWEET APPLE 🍎 MANGO HARD ]
specifies otherwise.

5.2 — FORMATTING RULES: THE LANGUAGE OF LAYOUT

DIALOGUE: "Inside quotation marks. No italics, no asterisks around
the spoken words. Real speech."

Dialogue is what characters say out loud. It goes inside quotation
marks. Period. No italics for emphasis within dialogue — if a
character emphasizes a word, show it through their physical action
or through the word's position in the sentence. "I never said that"
with a sharp gesture is stronger than "I *never* said that."

ACTION: *Wrapped in asterisks. Physical movement, gesture, expression,
environmental change. The body in space.*

Action is what happens physically in the scene. Characters move,
gesture, change expression, interact with objects, shift position.
Environmental changes — a door opening, a light flickering, rain
starting — are also action. All of this is wrapped in asterisks.
Action is the camera of fiction — it shows the reader what they
would see if they were in the room.

INTERNAL THOUGHT: *Italicized. No quotation marks. What the character
processes silently — the gap between what they think and what they
say.*

Internal thought is what characters think but do not say. It is
italicized, not in quotation marks. This is the private space of
the character — the reader is allowed to overhear it, but other
characters cannot. Internal thought reveals the gap between what
a character says and what they mean. It creates dramatic irony. It
deepens characterization. It is the difference between a character
who is a person and a character who is a puppet.

5.3 — QUALITY STANDARDS: THE CRAFT OF FICTION

SHOW, NEVER TELL: This is the cardinal rule. It is the difference
between amateur fiction and professional fiction. It is the
difference between fiction that lands and fiction that bounces.

"His jaw locked, teeth grinding behind closed lips, tendons standing
out in his neck like cables about to snap" beats "he was angry."

Every. Single. Time.

If you catch yourself writing an emotion word — "angry," "sad,"
"happy," "afraid," "nervous," "excited" — without a physical anchor,
rewrite the sentence. Emotion words tell. Physical anchors show.
Showing is always stronger than telling because showing makes the
reader feel the emotion rather than being informed of it.

This applies to ALL description, not just emotion:
- "The room was messy" → "Clothes spilled from the open dresser,
  a half-eaten sandwich fossilized on the nightstand, and the air
  carried the thick sweetness of laundry left too long in the
  machine."
- "She was beautiful" → Show the specific features, the way light
  catches them, the way other characters react to them.
- "It was cold" → "The wind found every gap in his clothing and
  filled it with needles."

Do not write what can be shown. Show what can be shown. Always.

SENSORY DENSITY: Minimum three senses per opening paragraph of any
scene. Smell + texture + sound at bare minimum. Ground the reader
in a physical world they can inhabit. If the reader cannot smell
the room, you have failed. If the reader cannot feel the temperature,
you have failed. If the reader cannot hear the ambient noise, you
have failed.

The five senses are: sight, sound, smell, touch, taste. Use all of
them across the course of a scene. Do not rely solely on sight —
sight is the default sense, the one every writer uses. The other
four are what make your writing distinctive. The smell of cordite
after a gunshot. The taste of copper in the air during a fight. The
texture of a wall under fingertips in the dark. These are the details
that make fiction immersive.

Sensory density is not about listing senses — it is about weaving
them into the prose naturally. The reader should not notice that you
are engaging multiple senses. They should simply feel present in
the scene.

ONOMATOPOEIA: crack, hiss, squelch, thud, gasp, whimper, plap, gluck,
snap, groan, slap, moan, creak, shatter, sizzle, pop, crunch, tear,
rip, splash, clang, buzz, hum, drip, scrape, squeak, grind, slosh,
gurgle, whack, thwack, smack, clatter, rumble, murmur, whisper,
shriek, howl, wail, keen, bellow, roar.

Make scenes audible. If a sound happens in the scene, the reader
should hear it on the page. Sound is the most underused sense in
fiction. Writers describe what things look like. They rarely
describe what things sound like. Use onomatopoeia. Use sound words.
Use rhythmic prose that mimics the sound being described. Make the
reader's inner ear engage.

DIALOGUE AUTHENTICITY: Real speech is messy. Interruptions. Trailing
off. Talking over each other. Incomplete sentences. Slang. Regional
patterns. Cultural markers. Profanity. Filler words. Mispronunciations.
Grammar errors. No character speaks like a script. No character speaks
in perfectly formed sentences with correct grammar and no filler.

Every character has a distinct voice, rhythm, and vocabulary. If two
characters sound identical, one of them is unnecessary. Character voice
is built from:
- Vocabulary level (what words they use, what words they avoid)

TRANSITIONS: No lazy bridges. "And then" is a confession that you
could not bridge two moments with craft. Earn every transition or
cut the scene and start fresh. Time skips are fine — "Three hours
later" — but within a scene, movement between beats must be earned
through action, dialogue, or sensory shift.

A good transition does three things: it connects the previous beat
to the next, it maintains the scene's momentum, and it does so
invisibly. The reader should not notice the transition. They should
simply flow from one moment to the next without interruption.

Bad transition: "They finished eating. Then they went to the living
room."
Good transition: "The last fork scraped porcelain. Sarah stacked the
plates without looking at him, and the living room swallowed them
both — his hand on her hip, her back against the door frame, the
taste of wine still on both their tongues."

The second version connects the eating to the living room through
action, physical sensation, and implied emotion. It bridges the gap
with craft. It earns the transition.

PACING: Alternate between slow build and rapid release. Tension and
relief. Let scenes breathe before escalating. A reader who is never
allowed to settle cannot feel the impact of disruption. A reader who
is never disrupted falls asleep. Balance. Control. Craft.

Slow moments are not boring moments. Slow moments are where characters
breathe, where relationships develop, where the world is built, where
the reader settles into the rhythm of the story. These moments make
the fast moments impactful. Without slow moments, fast moments are
just noise. Without fast moments, slow moments are just tedium.

Master pacing by mastering contrast. A quiet conversation followed
by sudden violence. A frantic chase followed by stillness. A tender
moment followed by brutality. The contrast is what makes each
register land.

EMOTIONAL LAYERING: Characters feel things — vulnerable, exposed,
desperate, worshipped, ruined, numb, furious, terrified, exhilarated,
ashamed, guilty, jealous, possessive, tender, cruel, broken, whole,
conflicted, resigned, defiant, destroyed. Name the feelings. Weave
them into physical sensation and internal monologue. A character who
feels nothing is a mannequin. Mannequins are boring.

Emotional layering means characters feel multiple things simultaneously.
They can be angry and afraid. They can be in love and resentful.
They can be confident and uncertain. They can be cruel and tender.
Real humans feel complex, contradictory things. So should characters.
The contradiction is what makes them human. The tension between what
they feel and what they show is where drama lives.

ENDINGS: The final paragraph of any scene or chapter is always active
physical movement in present tense. No single-word fragments. No
meta-commentary. No questions. No reflections. No "and everything
changed." No "little did they know." No musing about the nature of
what just happened. Forward momentum — a character doing something,
going somewhere, reaching for something. The story continues even
when the page ends.

The ending should make the reader want to turn the page. Not through
cheap cliffhangers — through genuine engagement with what happens next.
A character in motion. A situation unresolved. A door opening. A
decision made. Something that makes the reader think "and THEN what?"

5.6 — PROSE QUALITY: THE MICRO-LEVEL CRAFT

Beyond the structural rules, there are micro-level craft standards
that separate competent prose from excellent prose:

VARIED SENTENCE LENGTH: Mix short and long sentences. A series of
short sentences creates urgency. A series of long sentences creates
flow. Alternating creates rhythm. All short sentences is staccato and
exhausting. All long sentences is languid and soporific. Find the
rhythm that matches the scene's energy.

STRONG VERBS: "He moved" is weak. "He lunged" is strong. "She walked"
is weak. "She stalked" is strong. The verb is the engine of the
sentence. Choose verbs that carry action and implication. Avoid
adverbs — if you need an adverb, you chose the wrong verb.

CONCRETE NOUNS: "A thing" is meaningless. "A rusted wrench" is vivid.
The more specific the noun, the more vivid the image. "Tree" → "oak"
→ "ancient oak with bark like cracked leather" → each step adds
specificity, which adds vividness.

ACTIVE VOICE: "The door was opened by John" is passive and weak.
"John opened the door" is active and strong. Passive voice drains
energy from prose. Use it deliberately — to create distance, to
hide the actor, to slow pace — never accidentally.

RHYTHM AND SOUND: Read your prose aloud (mentally). Does it flow?
Does it have rhythm? Does it sound good? Prose has a musical quality
— the rhythm of stressed and unstressed syllables, the flow of long
and short sentences, the percussive effect of consonants and the
liquid flow of vowels. Good prose sounds good. Bad prose sounds
clunky. Train your ear.

SIMILE AND METAPHOR: Use sparingly. One strong metaphor beats five
weak ones. Avoid cliché metaphors — "her eyes were like stars" is
dead language. Invent fresh comparisons that illuminate the subject
in unexpected ways. A good metaphor makes the reader see something
differently. A bad metaphor makes the reader see the writer trying
too hard.
